# Hobby, maker and open-source prior art: screw-nut-pulls-chain/belt/cable joint drives

Scope: the design under study is a ball screw (SFU1204, 12 mm, 4 mm lead) lying along the arm link, driven 1:1 by a NEMA 17 on the link; the ball nut carries two #25 roller chains (one each side of the screw), each wrapping a 25-tooth sprocket fixed to the next joint, round an idler and back to the nut; about 64 mm of nut travel gives about 145 degrees.

Verification tags used below:
- "(page read)" = I opened the page and the fact is on it.
- "(search summary only)" = the fact came from the search tool's summary of the page; I did not open the page, so treat it as unverified.
- Reddit could not be searched (the search tool refuses reddit.com) and YouTube results did not come back, so neither is covered.

## Q1. Are there DIY/open-source arms where a lead/ball screw nut pulls a chain, timing belt or cable wrapped around a joint pulley?

### Takeaway
I found no hobby, maker or open-source robot arm that does exactly this (screw nut pulling a chain, belt or cable round a joint sprocket/pulley); searches of hackaday.io/hackaday.com, Instructables, Printables/Thingiverse, Chief Delphi (FRC), the LinuxCNC forum and Eurobricks turned up only the neighbouring families. The same mechanism does exist outside the hobby world: a granted Samsung patent (ball screw driven by a belt, nut carries a closed steel-cable loop round an output pulley and idle pulley) and a 2010 Eindhoven humanoid thesis that drew it up as an "indirect ball screw" concept and rejected it. The nearest open-source hardware is a different scale and joint type: a prosthetic hand whose linear actuators pull finger tendons.

### Cited Findings
**Strong matches found by chance (patent / academic; other researchers cover these, listed because they match closely):**
- Samsung Electronics, US 8,635,929 B2, "Robot joint driving apparatus, robot having the same and cable linkage method of robot joint driving apparatus"; priority 14 Jan 2010 (KR10-2010-0003518), filed 10 Jan 2011, granted 28 Jan 2014. A motor that runs both ways drives a ball screw through a driving belt. The movable member (nut) is screw-connected to the ball screw and kept from turning by guide bearings in a vertical slot in the frame. A steel cable is fixed to both sides of the member, made into a closed loop (ends joined by a loop sleeve) and wrapped round a disc-shaped output pulley and an idle pulley under set tension. Guide pins on the member hang the cable in loops so that four parallel lines run between the pulleys and the member. (page read) — [Google Patents US8635929B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8635929B2/en)
- Same patent, stated problem and fix: the joint's torsional stiffness (how far it moves under external force when stopped) needed raising. Bigger pulleys enlarge the apparatus, and a shorter free cable length means a shorter ball screw, which reduces the joint's movable angle. Their fix is to run the cable in parallel more than once, like springs in parallel. Slip at the output pulley is stopped by fixing loop sleeves held by spacers, with adhesive and cable pretension. (page read) — [Google Patents US8635929B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8635929B2/en)
- A related patent, US 8,459,139 "Robot joint driving apparatus and robot having the same", puts an idle pulley in a ball-screw-and-wire transmission to cut the wire tension applied to the movable member. (search summary only; assignee not checked) — [Justia US8459139](https://patents.justia.com/patent/8459139)
- Maarten Dekker, "Mechanical design of a humanoid robot's lower body" (TU Eindhoven / Dutch Robotics, successor to the TUlip RoboCup humanoid, 2010). Appendix F, concept F.2 "Indirect ball screw" for the hip joint: a DC motor and ball screw are fixed to the waist, the nut is linearly guided and kept from turning, and a steel wire rope fixed to the nut is redirected by pulleys to a disc on the hip tube, so the nut's linear motion turns the disc. "The steel wire rope is fourfold applied, to prevent the nut from additional moment loads (the forces apply to the center of the ball screw nut)". (page read; PDF text extracted) — [Dekker 2010 thesis PDF](https://techunited.nl/media/files/humanoid/MaartenDekker_GRAD2010_Mechanical_design_of_a_humanoid_robots_lower_body.pdf)
- Same thesis, its concept comparison table for "Ball screw + steel wire rope": stiffness 4 kNm/rad (marked with an asterisk, i.e. an estimate), efficiency 0.90, backlash 0. It was rejected because it "is more complex and incorporates more components in order to load the ball screw in center of the ball screw nut", which costs compactness and would obstruct the torso joint's range. A planetary gearbox plus a final gear set was chosen instead. (page read) — [Dekker 2010 thesis PDF](https://techunited.nl/media/files/humanoid/MaartenDekker_GRAD2010_Mechanical_design_of_a_humanoid_robots_lower_body.pdf)
- Same thesis, measured data on the older TUlip robot, which ran a steel wire rope over two pulleys after a Maxon planetary gearbox at hip, knee and ankle: the rope cut final backlash from about 1 degree (the gearbox alone) to about 0.2 degrees. Rope Ø2 mm, length about 350 mm, stiffness 1.9 kN/mm, end pulley r = 50 mm, giving 4.7 kNm/rad; the author notes real stiffness is lower because the redirecting pulleys are not counted. (page read) — [Dekker 2010 thesis PDF](https://techunited.nl/media/files/humanoid/MaartenDekker_GRAD2010_Mechanical_design_of_a_humanoid_robots_lower_body.pdf)
- ADDCO, Inc., US 5,850,579, pan/tilt support with concentric drive shafts: a tilt belt turns the lead screw's straight-line motion into rotation of the camera plate; the lead screw and nut self-lock against outside forces tilting the camera. (search summary only) — [FreePatentsOnline US5850579](https://www.freepatentsonline.com/5850579.html)
- US 9,149,370, powered artificial knee with agonist-antagonist actuation: a ball nut moving along a screw pulls a loop mounted on a knee joint pulley, turning the nut's linear force into knee torque. (search summary only; assignee and date not checked) — [USPTO PDF 9149370](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9149370)

### Inferences
- The arm design under study is, as far as I could find, new in the hobby/open-source world, but the mechanism itself (screw nut pulling a closed tension loop round a joint pulley, with an idler) is established in Samsung's patent and in Dekker's thesis. Both of those used steel wire rope; neither used roller chain on a sprocket.
- Two chains, one each side of the screw, solve the same problem that Samsung's "four parallel lines" and Dekker's "fourfold" rope solve: keeping the pull centred on the nut so the ball screw does not see a tilting moment. Both sources treat that as necessary.
- Both strong matches name stiffness of the tension member as the main weakness, and both note the trade-off between pulley size, free length and joint angle. With chain the analogous items are chain stretch/elastic span and sprocket polygon effect; the free span between nut, sprocket and idler changes as the nut moves.
- Dekker rejected the concept for packaging (too many parts to keep the load centred), not for performance; his table rated it at zero backlash and 0.90 efficiency, comparable to a direct ball screw.

- Reddit (r/robotics, r/AskEngineers, r/3Dprinting) could not be searched with the tools available; YouTube searches returned nothing usable. A hobby build of this exact kind could exist there.
- No builder, year or URL found for any hobby arm with a screw nut pulling chain/belt/cable round a joint sprocket.

- Moveo, Dexter (Haddington), SO-100/SO-101 and uFactory/Dobot desktop arms: drive types not checked this session (the Moveo GitHub page did not describe them).

## Q2. Which hobby arms use lead/ball screws in other ways (screw driving a linkage like an excavator cylinder, slider-crank, linear-actuator struts)?

### Takeaway
This family is common. The clearest hobby examples are Homofaciens' wooden robot arm (salvaged CNC lead screws as pivoting struts), James Bruton's openDog and Sonic robots (ball screws in legs), a lead-screw arm on Medium, a 3D-printable N20 lead-screw excavator cylinder on GitHub, LEGO Technic linear actuators in excavator sets, and FRC arms driven by lead-screw push rods. The Eindhoven thesis chose this direct, pivoting-strut layout for its humanoid knee. All of these push a lever, so torque varies with angle and range is limited (about 100 to 150 degrees direct), unlike a chain wrapped on a sprocket.

### Cited Findings
- Homofaciens (Norbert Heinz) robot arm v1.0: frame of scrap wooden roof battens; motion from steppers and 8 mm lead screws salvaged from an old CNC build; brass nuts mounted on a batten subframe; "two extra pivot points at the drive" keep screw and motor aligned as the joint moves; joints turn about 100 degrees; the base joint uses 10 mm threaded rod for stiffness; leverage depends on the screw pitch and the distance from the joint pivot to the spindle mount (shorter = faster, longer = more force/precision); the spindles wobble because screw and motor are not perfectly aligned, accepted for pick-and-place since moves repeat; it stacks 11 M8 nuts; joint backlash set by two adjustment screws. Page undated. (page read) — [homofaciens.de robot arm v1.0](https://homofaciens.de/technics-machines-robot-arm-v1-0_en.htm)
- Hackaday covered it on 19 Aug 2021 (Dan Maloney): "Steppers and lead screws cannibalized from an old CNC build"; "It's a bit — compliant — but precise enough to pick up nuts and stack them nicely"; a commenter likens it to a backhoe with screws in place of cylinders. (page read) — [Hackaday: Minimalist robot arm really stacks up](https://hackaday.com/2021/08/19/minimalist-robot-arm-really-stacks-up/)
- James Bruton (XRobots), openDog, 2018: chose ball screws over planetary gearboxes because of backlash he had in his BB-9E project (as he had for his exoskeleton); three motors per leg. A commenter argued ball screws are rigid and heavy and could break under unexpected external force. The article does not describe the linkage geometry. (page read) — [Hackaday 2018-06-11: James Bruton is making a dog](https://hackaday.com/2018/06/11/james-bruton-is-making-a-dog-opendog-project/)
- James Bruton, Sonic self-balancing robot, 2020: its knees use a pair of motors with ball screws, which are not back-drivable. (search summary only) — [Hackaday 2020-01-31](https://hackaday.com/2020/01/31/sonic-the-hedgehog-self-balancing-robot-can-bend-at-the-knees/)
- Hackster also covered a budget linear actuator from James Bruton (title only seen) — [Hackster: Budget Linear Actuator from James Bruton](https://www.hackster.io/news/budget-linear-actuator-from-james-bruton-4f13e41c35ee)
- "Creating a Robot Arm using a Linear Actuator Pt.1" (Medium, author handle saivivekvenna): the first joint uses a lead screw on the end of a mini CIM motor shaft, replacing the hydraulics of a similar arm; an eccentric spin of the actuator caused heavy vibration until the coupling was fixed. - LEGO Technic linear actuators (design 43097; in sets such as Excavator 8294 and Telescopic Handler 8295) are screw actuators that imitate hydraulic cylinders and hold position without back-driving; builders report the internal clutch slipping under load and pin friction under bending load. (search summary only) — [Eurobricks: abilities of LEGO linear actuators](https://www.eurobricks.com/forum/forums/topic/117588-help-abilities-of-lego-linear-actuators); [Eurobricks: LAs slipping](https://www.eurobricks.com/forum/forums/topic/59646-las-slipping-need-help); [BrickOwl 43097](https://www.brickowl.com/catalog/lego-linear-actuator-43097)

### Inferences
- In this family the screw is a pivoting strut (both ends on pivots, like a hydraulic cylinder), so the motor or screw has to swing with the joint (Dekker needed two universal joints; Homofaciens needed extra pivot points), and the lever ratio changes over the stroke. The chain-wrap design keeps the screw fixed along the link and gives a constant ratio (constant sprocket radius) over its full range.
- Ranges reported for direct struts are about 100 degrees (Homofaciens) to about 150 degrees (the Gleyzes solar tracker in Q3, direct mount); going further needs an extra linkage (the excavator bucket "dog-bone"). The chain-wrap design's roughly 145 degrees is set only by nut travel and sprocket size.
- The common reported problems are misalignment wobble, compliance, and non-back-drivability. The chain design trades the pivot and alignment problem for tension-member stiffness and tensioning.

### Gaps
- Several well-known excavator-style hobby arms (e.g. Firgelli/Progressive Automations linear-actuator arm tutorials, YouTube RC excavator builds) were not checked.
- How openDog's ball screws connect to the legs (lever, belt, pulley) was not confirmed from a page this session.

## Q3. 3D-printer/CNC-community projects that turn a pivot with a screw (camera sliders/pan-tilt, telescope mounts, solar trackers, animatronics)

### Takeaway
Screw-turned pivots are long established in amateur astronomy (barn-door trackers, tangent-arm drives) and appear in DIY solar trackers. In these the nut pushes an arm (tangent/lever) instead of pulling a wrapped band or chain, so they have tangent error over large angles. The one belt-wrap screw pivot found in the camera world is a commercial patent (ADDCO pan/tilt). Hobby camera pan-tilt heads use plain belt reductions.

### Cited Findings
- td0g "Guided Telescope Mount & Accessories (Tracker V3)" (Tyler, posted 5 Jan 2019, updated 17 Sep 2019): a modified barn-door drive with "two long arms and a lead screw to push the arms away from each other"; two ACME nuts screw onto a driven pulley held in bearings (rotating-nut design), driven by a NEMA 17 through a 16:85 belt; the RA drive needs a lookup table and varying speed because motor speed and shaft rotation are non-linearly related; RA runs about 3 hours before reset. (page read) — [td0g Tracker V3](https://td0g.ca/2019/01/05/guided-telescope-mount-accessories-tracker-v3-diy/)
- Heavy-duty tangent arm drive (Mike Oates, Scotch mount project): a 1 rev/50 s motor drives a 20 TPI threaded steel rod against a tangent arm of about 348 mm radius, giving a reasonably accurate sidereal rate. (search summary only; the site refused connection) — [mikeoates.org Heavy Duty Tangent Arm Drive](http://www.mikeoates.org/mas/projects/scotch/hdm/home.htm)
- Tangent-arm drives: a nut on a motor-driven lead screw moves a radial arm through an arc; the position error follows the tangent of the arm angle, which US 5,327,062 adds a compensation mechanism to correct. (search summary only) — [USPTO PDF 5327062](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/5327062)
- JP Gleyzes, low-cost solar panel MPPT sun tracker (hackaday.io log, 25 Sep 2022): replaced a stepper worm-gear azimuth drive (1472 turns per 180 degrees, limited torque, stepper stalled in wind) with a leadscrew linear actuator acting through two arms on a crank, "directly inspired from excavators"; the leadscrew holds position unpowered; target 240 degrees of azimuth (more than 180 "theoretically" possible), against about 150 degrees with a directly mounted actuator. (page read) — [hackaday.io: a new Azimut mechanics](https://hackaday.io/project/185105-low-cost-solar-panel-solution-mppt-sun-tracker/log/211324-a-new-azimut-mechanics)
- Commercial/vendor DIY solar tracker guides use one linear actuator per axis and note that longer moment arms cut actuator force but need more stroke (vendor content). (search summary only) — [Firgelli: DIY dual-axis solar tracker](https://www.firgelliauto.com/blogs/tutorials/maximize-energy-how-to-build-a-dual-axis-diy-solar-tracker)
- A Solar Panel Talk forum thread suggests a sprocket on a gearmotor's slow shaft driving bicycle chain for a home-made tracker (gearmotor, not screw). (search summary only) — [Solar Panel Talk: home made dual axis solar tracker](https://www.solarpaneltalk.com/forum/solar/the-pros-and-cons-of-solar-energy/411101-home-made-dual-axis-solar-tracker)
- Camera pan/tilt: ADDCO US 5,850,579 converts lead-screw motion to tilt through a belt (see Q1). Hobby pan-tilt heads use stepper belt reductions, e.g. about 15:160 teeth on pan and 15:60 on tilt in one Hackster build. (search summary only) — [Hackster: pan and tilt head with very slow and fluid movements](https://www.hackster.io/daniel232/pan-and-tilt-head-with-very-slow-and-fluid-movements-43c5a2); [FreePatentsOnline US5850579](https://www.freepatentsonline.com/5850579.html)
- A design guide (date not checked) notes a timing belt round pulleys as a simple linear-to-rotary conversion and recommends timing belts over V-belts, which it says add 3 to 5 degrees of position uncertainty. (search summary only; vendor knowledge-base page) — [Industrial Monitor Direct: linear to rotary motion conversion](https://industrialmonitordirect.com/blogs/knowledgebase/linear-to-rotary-motion-conversion-mechanisms-and-design-guide)

### Inferences
- Amateur telescope makers accept the screw-pushes-arm layout because they only need a few degrees, or they correct it in software (td0g's lookup table). For a 145-degree arm joint, the wrapped chain avoids tangent error entirely: angle is travel divided by sprocket pitch radius.
- The Gleyzes tracker shows the same range limit seen in Q2 (about 150 degrees direct) and the same excavator-linkage workaround.

### Gaps
- No animatronics example was found (not searched in depth).
- No camera slider or pan-tilt hobby project using a screw-pulled belt round a tilt pulley was found; only the ADDCO patent.
- No amateur telescope "band drive" (screw-pulled steel band round a sector or drum) was found, although the search was for it.

## Q4. For the closest few: tension member, screw, nut attachment, joint range, motor location, reported problems

### Takeaway
The two closest (Samsung's patent and Dekker's concept) both use steel cable or rope, a belt or motor driving a ball screw, multiple parallel strands attached to the nut to keep the load centred, and an idle or redirecting pulley. Both name tension-member stiffness as the main issue. None of the found items uses roller chain on a sprocket, which is the distinctive part of the design under study.

### Inferences
- How the design under study differs from the closest matches: (1) #25 roller chain on a 25-tooth sprocket instead of steel cable on a smooth pulley, so the drive is positive (no slip and no adhesive or fixing sleeve needed) but gains chordal (polygon) effect and chain stretch; (2) two separate chains either side of the screw instead of one cable looped into 4 lines, which centres the load the same way; (3) motor and screw ride on the link they drive from, as in Dekker's concept (actuation in the parent link); (4) it is a hobby/printed build, whereas the matches are a corporate patent and an unbuilt thesis concept.
- Arithmetic check for the caller (my calculation, not from a source): a 25-tooth #25 sprocket (6.35 mm pitch) has a pitch diameter of 6.35 / sin(7.2 deg) = about 50.7 mm (radius about 25.3 mm). 64 mm of travel / 25.3 mm = 2.53 rad = about 145 degrees, which matches the brief. But with a 1:1 belt and a 4 mm lead, one motor turn moves the nut 4 mm = about 9.05 degrees of joint, i.e. about 39.8:1, not 119:1. 119:1 would need about a 3:1 belt reduction (or about a 1.33 mm lead). Worth checking which figure is right.
- Stiffness reference point from Dekker: a Ø2 mm steel rope 350 mm long at r = 50 mm gave 4.7 kNm/rad. The equivalent for this design is the chain's elastic stiffness over the free spans (nut to sprocket to idler) at r = 25.3 mm; because rotational stiffness scales with r squared, a 25 mm radius needs about 4 times the linear stiffness of a 50 mm radius to match. This should be measured or calculated for #25 chain; I found no figure.

### Gaps
- No reported field problems (stretch, wear, binding) for a chain-on-sprocket version of this drive; none was found to report on.
- No #25 roller chain axial stiffness figure was found in this scope.
- Joint ranges and motor locations for the Samsung, ADDCO and US 9,149,370 patents were not given or not checked.
