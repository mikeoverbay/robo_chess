# Roller chain replaces cable in old drive

No robot, patent, product or hobby build found in this scan uses your drive as built. It is a known cable mechanism with roller chain in place of the cable. The loop itself, a belt to a ball screw with the nut carrying a closed tension loop around a joint pulley and an idler and back, has been built or patented with steel cable or polymer tendon since the late 1990s. Examples are MIT's Spring Flamingo walker, a 2003 Chongqing University patent that turns a joint about 160°, CEA's screw-and-cable actuators, and Samsung's US8635929B2, which Google lists as in force until about November 2031. Roller chain pulled in a straight line around a sprocket is also old, but only in hydraulic piston-chain rotary actuators and backhoe swing drives from the 1960s on. No source shows the two together: a screw-driven nut pulling roller chain around a sprocket to turn a robot joint, with two chains flanking the screw and the motor and screw riding on the link. Each of those features exists somewhere on its own. The combination does not appear. With 1:1 GT2 belts the drive is **39.8:1** (4 mm lead on a 25.33 mm sprocket pitch radius), about a third to a quarter of Spring Flamingo's 111–159:1. The 119:1 in the earlier briefs belonged to the 3:1-belt version. Two blind spots remain. Chinese and Japanese utility models were barely covered, and Reddit and YouTube could not be searched. A closer match is most likely to be in one of those.

## Cable versions of this loop go back to 1990s walking robots

The nearest working machine is **Spring Flamingo** (MIT Leg Lab, Jerry Pratt's 2000 thesis). Its drive is the same as yours in order: a DC motor, a 2.5:1 timing belt, a ball screw of 3.175 mm lead, and an output cable that "winds around an idler pulley and travels to the joint" ([Pratt 2000](https://www.ftp.ai.mit.edu/pub/users/jpratt/phd_thesis.pdf)). The joint pulleys are 44 mm (hip and ankle) and 64 mm (knee). From the thesis's force and speed figures the ratios work out to **about 111:1 and 159:1**, with roughly ±45° at the hip and 90° at the knee. It differs from yours in three ways. It uses cable, it adds series springs for force control, and all six actuators sit in the body with cables routed down the leg. MIT CSAIL's Domo arm used the same ball-screw-to-cable stage and reported **cable breakage at the crimps** as a known failure ([Edsinger-Gonzales & Weber 2004](https://groups.csail.mit.edu/lbr/mars/pubs/humanoids04.pdf)).

The patents follow the same pattern. **Chongqing University CN1272147C** (filed 2003, lapsed 2008) uses a belt to turn a nut, so the screw translates. A steel rope from both ends of the screw wraps 270° around an output sheave, pinned through a hole, then runs to a follower sheave. The joint turns **about 160°** ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/CN1272147C/en)). **Samsung US8635929B2** (priority 2010, granted 2014) is the closest patent. A belt drives a ball screw. A guided movable member on the screw carries a closed steel-cable loop around an idle pulley and an output pulley that drives the joint directly. Guide pins make the loop run as four parallel lines for torsional stiffness, and sleeves, spacers, adhesive and pretension stop slip at the output pulley ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8635929B2/en)). Google lists it as **active**, with the 12th-year fee paid in July 2025 and an estimated expiry of 2031-11-24. Its claim 1 is built on a rectilinear movable member and a "cable assembly" with lines in parallel. The ball screw appears only in dependent claims 6–7. Whether two roller chains fall under "cable assembly" is a legal question. This scan is not a freedom-to-operate search, and Google's status labels are its own estimate, not register data.

CEA-LIST's **Screw-and-Cable System** turns the screw arrangement around. The nut rotates, a hollow screw translates with no rail, and the cable runs through the screw's bore. It is built into the limb segment, as yours is. It drove the 4-axis ABLE exoskeleton, with 110° on one axis ([Jarrasse, Garrec et al., IROS 2008](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/181352/files/04651014-1.pdf?version=1)). A Haption presentation shows it in the circa-2001 Virtuose 6D haptic arm; this came from a garbled search summary and is unverified ([Haption, LIRMM](https://www.lirmm.fr/uee07/presentations/forum/HaptionLIRMM.pdf)). The US patent, US7389974B2, lapsed in 2020 ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7389974B2/en)). Maarten Dekker's 2010 TU Eindhoven humanoid thesis drew up an "indirect ball screw" hip with steel rope run fourfold so the pull acts at the nut centre. He rated it **0 backlash, 0.90 efficiency and about 4 kNm/rad** (an estimate), then rejected it as too many parts and too bulky ([Dekker 2010](https://techunited.nl/media/files/humanoid/MaartenDekker_GRAD2010_Mechanical_design_of_a_humanoid_robots_lower_body.pdf)). **Ekso US10702441B2** (2016 priority, now expired for unpaid fees) brings opposed cords back parallel to the ball screw over guide pulleys so the nut needs no side-load rails ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10702441B2/en)). The MIT agonist-antagonist knee and the Robonaut 2 hand use belt- or motor-driven ball screws pulling steel cable or Vectran tendon ([US 8,500,823](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8500823); [Bridgwater et al. 2012](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20110023122/downloads/20110023122.pdf)). None of them uses chain.

## Pulled roller chain is 1960s hydraulic practice, never screw-driven

Chain wrapped on a sprocket and pulled in a straight line is an old industrial rotary actuator, and every version found is fluid-driven. In the piston-chain type, roller chain is fixed to both sides of a power piston and wraps the output sprocket. One variant runs a closed chain over two sprockets with an idler for tension, reaches **five full turns and 23,500 lb-in**, and is limited by chain and sprocket strength ([Power & Motion, Ch. 19](https://www.powermotiontech.com/technologies/other-technologies/article/21882754/book-2-chapter-19-rotary-actuators); [Hydraulics & Pneumatics](https://www.hydraulicspneumatics.com/200/TechZone/FluidPowerAcces/Article/False/9517/TechZone-FluidPowerAcces)). The patent line runs from Graham's 1960s US3267816A ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US3267816A/en)) through backhoe swing drives such as US4078680, where two rams each pull a chain on a common drum ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US4078680)). It continues to Hiflex US6860189B2 (2001, lapsed 2013), where pressurised tubes pull a chain over a sprocket ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6860189B2/en)). A later valve-actuator patent describes the chain loop tied to cylinder rods as a known method kept to special applications ([US7607640](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7607640)). All of these are long expired.

The only chain-and-sprocket item with an electric-style linear actuator is **Suncore's solar tracker, US8536504B2**. A linear actuator's moving coupling anchors two chains that wrap a sprocket on the torque tube and a smaller second sprocket, for over 180° ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8536504B2/en)). The text fetched does not say whether that actuator is a screw, and it is a fixed post, not a joint on a moving link. Screw-driven quarter-turn valve actuators exist but use cranks or travelling blocks, not chain ([US5085401](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5085401)). So the ram-to-screw swap is the open variable on the chain side, and this scan found no patent claiming it.

## Hobby arms use screws as struts, not wraps

Searches of hackaday.io, Hackaday, Instructables, Printables, Thingiverse, Chief Delphi, the LinuxCNC forum and Eurobricks found no hobby or open-source arm with a screw nut pulling chain, belt or cable around a joint sprocket. Where hobbyists use screws, the screw is a pivoting strut, like an excavator cylinder. Homofaciens' wooden arm uses salvaged 8 mm CNC lead screws, needs extra pivots to keep motor and screw aligned, and gets **about 100°** with visible wobble ([homofaciens.de](https://homofaciens.de/technics-machines-robot-arm-v1-0_en.htm)). Gleyzes' solar tracker got about 150° from a direct-mounted leadscrew and needed an excavator-style linkage to reach 240° ([hackaday.io](https://hackaday.io/project/185105-low-cost-solar-panel-solution-mppt-sun-tracker/log/211324-a-new-azimut-mechanics)). Dekker's chosen knee design is the same type: a direct ball screw between two universal joints, for a 135° requirement ([Dekker 2010](https://techunited.nl/media/files/humanoid/MaartenDekker_GRAD2010_Mechanical_design_of_a_humanoid_robots_lower_body.pdf)). Telescope barn-door drives push an arm with a screw and need a lookup table to correct the non-linear rate ([td0g Tracker V3](https://td0g.ca/2019/01/05/guided-telescope-mount-accessories-tracker-v3-diy/)). FRC teams drive arm pivots with #25 chain on a large sprocket, but from a gearmotor. One 2007 team drove its arm with a lead-screw push rod instead and noted that it avoided a large sprocket and chain ([Chief Delphi](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/high-torque-arm-design-questions/137836)). Popular open arms such as PAROL6 use belts and planetary gearboxes ([hackaday.io PAROL6](https://hackaday.io/project/191860/log/222436-parol6-mechanical-structure)).

The wrap removes the strut's two problems. The ratio is constant, because joint angle is nut travel divided by 25.33 mm, with no tangent error. Range is set by travel and sprocket size, not by linkage geometry. Your 145° matches the top of what direct struts manage and needs no swinging motor.

## What is new is the chain, and it brings chain problems

Taking the features one at a time, only the first is unmatched. **Roller chain on a toothed sprocket, pulled by a screw nut, to turn a joint** appears in no source found. Two **separate chains flanking the screw** are new hardware for an old purpose. Dekker's fourfold rope and Ekso's parallel cords exist to keep the pull centred on the nut so the screw sees no tilting moment. Samsung's four parallel lines were added for torsional stiffness. **Motor and screw on the link** are not new on their own. ABLE embeds its drive in the limb segment, Dekker's concept puts it on the parent link, and Boston Dynamics' screw actuator puts the screw inside the upper leg, though it drives a linkage, not a wrap ([uspto.report US10253855](https://uspto.report/patent/grant/10253855)). **All-steel, no series spring** marks it as a position drive. Spring Flamingo, Domo and the MIT knee built spring-in-series force drives.

The chain answers the failures the cable designs reported. A toothed mesh cannot slip, which Samsung treated with sleeves and adhesive. It has no crimps to break, as Domo's did, and it does not creep, which made Robonaut drop Spectra tendon. In exchange it brings three costs the cable record does not. First, chordal action: on 25 teeth the effective radius swings between R·cos 7.2° and R, a **0.79% ratio ripple** once per pitch. One 6.35 mm pitch of nut travel is **14.4° of joint**. Second, pin and bushing clearance and wear stretch add lost motion unless the idler holds a preload on the loop. Third, stiffness scales with radius squared. Dekker calculated 4.7 kNm/rad for the TUlip robot's Ø2 mm rope (1.9 kN/mm) on a 50 mm radius pulley, and noted the real figure is lower because the redirecting pulleys were left out. The same linear stiffness at 25.33 mm gives only **about 1.2 kNm/rad**, so the chain spans need about 3.9 times that linear stiffness to match. No #25 chain stiffness figure was found. Nut axial play also passes straight to the joint: **0.01 mm of play is 0.023° of joint**. A ball-screw-plus-pulley stage is typically above 90% efficient, and one complete belt-screw-pushrod actuator measured 77% ([Paine & Sentis](https://sites.utexas.edu/hcrl/wp-content/uploads/sites/3888/2016/01/prismatic-sea-paine.pdf)). No efficiency figure was found for roller chain used as a partial-wrap linear-to-rotary stage in a robot.

## Conclusion

The cable designs show what went wrong with this layout: stiffness and packaging (Samsung's reason for four lines, Dekker's reason to reject it) and cable ends (Domo). Chain removes slip and crimp failure. Its own weak points at a 25 mm radius are chordal ripple, pin clearance and span stiffness, and nobody has published a number for any of them in this use. The most useful thing to add is measurement: joint torsional stiffness in kNm/rad and lost motion at the sprocket, set against Dekker's 4.7 kNm/rad rope figure. Two more checks matter before calling the design new in public. A manual search of Chinese and Japanese utility models (丝杠, 链条, 链轮, 关节; ボールねじ, チェーン, スプロケット) is the search most likely to turn up a closer match. Samsung's still-active cable-loop patent is the one document a patent attorney would need to read against a chain version.
